<h2 style="text-align: left;" id="overview">Overview</h2>

Shortly after joining Night School, I was afforded the opportunity to start work on Unhinged from its earliest concepting. The Netflix game streaming platform was gaining momentum, and the team was looking to prove out what new types of experiences we could craft.

Unhinged evolved from a prototype that had been iterating on a narrative exploration horror game with a more traditional game controller control scheme. This gave us a solid foundation to build upon as we got Unhinged stood up, and introduced a more interactive controller as a core pillar.

Unhinged was built upon on timeline based animations that were played through in world interaction. Selecting an interactable would either play a small cinematic and leave you at your current "node", or navigate you to a new node. This allowed us to let our animators shine and craft polished, cinematic movement through the game, and our designers script interactions and navigation through the various nodes.

Our project was a monorepo containing both the game logic that ran on the cloud platform, as well as the controller logic that ran in a separate controller application running using Unity WebGL. The studios internal tooling was in Unity, and made it a great fit to toss in another running instance of a game and keep developers entierly within the same tooling for both main game and controller development. 

Each target built amd was delievered to their own platform through CI, and ran simultaneously in editor by opening multiple game windows. This allowed developers to test out functionality without needing to connect a phone, or update a build prior to testing.

This all got delivered onto the Netflix cloud streaming platform, where after selecting Unhinged in Netflix, you could simply scan a QR code and your phone became the game controller for Unhinged, providing an interactive simulated phone experience and gyro control of the game session.


<h2 style="text-align: left;" id="features">Feature Breakdowns</h2>
<h3 style="text-align: left;" id="ngc">Controller Application, Cross Device Communication and Simulation</h3>
<br>
Unhinged was comprised of three domains to separated assets, code, and content based on their need from within a domain.

* Game
* Controller
* Shared

We configured our assembly definitions and addressables to correctly separate content, includes, and executing code per domain. 

We were awaiting an upcoming input feature from the platform, but wanted to get prototyping as soon as possible, so the first feature of the controller I built was reading the gyro information from the phone, and communicating back to the main game instance to drive look and aim. This was later replaced with the platform integration of this feature, but by building our own, we were able to gain months of time. 

We offered both voice calls to and from NPC's, as well as a number of SMS contacts that you could have conversations with.
> I wrote the first "SMS" conversation to test the system I built, and made it a spam "we are contacting you about your cars extended warranty" message.

![Controller](/assets/vid/unhinged_controller.gif)

Phone Calls were a logical extension of the narrative tooling that Night School had already built, we simply expanded our dialogue line class to also support dialogue speakers that were on a different device. When one was encountered, it simply forwarded a message to the controller, and the audio would play as intended.

SMS required a bit more work as we wanted the player to be able to select multiple choices and have the NPC respond appropriately. I worked with our design and narrative teams, and built a data driven SMS feature that allowed messages to be defined, responses added, and configurable branching functionality. To ensure no issues arose through infinite loops, missing messages, or otherwise errant configuration, I wrote tests to ensure that SMS conversations would always resolve to a complete conversation.

Along with the Phone and SMS applications, the controller application displayed a dynamic interact button that responded to data on the various interactions being highlighted by the player.

I also integrated our Save/Load and checkpointing systems, as well as the controller disconnect and reconnection flow. 

While save/load is a pretty well worn path, due to the nature of mobile connections and the more interactive nature of our controller, we needed to ensure that a drop in communication with the controller would both pause the game and allow for a graceful reconnect to preserve the state of the game.
i.e. Reconnecting active calls and repopulating SMS histories.

Because of the network connection considerations, I elected to build the controller application using a Heirarchical State Machine, which made returning to the previous state was simply a manner of reloading the data, and returning to the gameplay.


>The former IT guy in me knows that somehow we all end up disconnected from wifi at some point or another, and wanted to make it easy to resolve if your connection drops or is unstable.

One of the other big parts of working with this mobile device controller was having good tooling and simulation within our editor. I setup editor tooling and configurations that let us selectively test immediately on device, or with another viewport running the controller application using mouse input as a simulation for the gyro aiming.


<h3 style="text-align: left;" id="gameplay">Gameplay: Cameras, Controls, Navigation, Interaction</h3>

Our core game loop roughly involved the following stages:

* Input
* Interaction with the world
* Navigation through the world.
* Game State Updating as a result of the above.
* Visual and Audio Effects as a response to the above.

The Gyro Input had aim magnetism to help reduce needing to be overly precise with holding at an exact spot to click on an interactable. This was able to be controlled for more and less impact at various moments, and on different interactions. Simply a data value to configure.

These interactions would navigate you through the world, and update the game state with your new node position when appropriate.

Unhinged's animations were a combination of motion capture performance, and hand authored animations. As you navigate Ava through the apartments, the camera moves and character interactions had all been carefully setup and tuned with specific amounts of free look and blending into areas of focus when required. 

These systems were managed by a central event bus that ran with the above stages, starting with processing communication with the controller application. It helped prevent the entire category of "this only happens when x happens before y" style bugs. 

> Unhinged was fun to look at the data on due to the node based nature of the navigation through the world. We could see how far the average player progressed, and how big moments landed, and what paths players were taking through the world.

**Spoilers** In the final moments of gameplay, we wanted to give the player a chance to finally get even with their attacker. As Ava pulls nails from her hand and reloads the nailgun used to hold her in place, the player is given direct control of the nailgun and must aim and fire at their attacker.

A small team of myself a designer and an animator put together this features. A blendspace is the logical fit for the free aim, and with a bit of gameplay sequencing to enable and disable it at the right time in the cinematic, we could ensure a smooth transition from the cinematic into and back out of free aim.

I really liked subverting the expectation at the end of the game, and giving the player that direct control in a new way.

<h3 style="text-align: left;" id="tests">Tests, CI, and QOL Tooling</h3>

CI and tests became a huge time and effort save on Unhinged. Alongside the SMS tests previously mentioned, I wrote and implemented tests that:

* Validated all interactions at our various Nodes.
    - Ensure all interactions contained valid data.
    - Checked for errant entries or null interaction entries, and warned developers at any found.

* Allowed a QA dev to record their interactions, and then replay their game session with the click of a button.
    - Prevented sequence breaks
    - Also allowed for input fuzzing, to catch timing specific or other tough to reproduce issues.

* Checked for unintended asset usage across game domains.

> I cannot overstate how much I love good CI tools and tests. There is nothing better than seeing a "The tests caught a bug!" message in slack.

With the above tests already written, extending a bit to kick out spreadsheets with data on all of the nodes and interactions was only a little more work.
This helped teams to validate their configuration and track assets through various stages of development (Scratch, Temp, Final).

Towards the end of development, as we pivoted to more optimization, I worked with our Graphics Engineer to take the Gameplay Input Fuzzing logic and run tests on our platform. We took the gameplay sessions and captured performance data to identify areas of high rendering cost. Then we could target exactly when a scene needed some additional work to better cooperate with the unique platform considerations of the virtualized environment.

### Things I'd Do Again
- Early focus on data model and game state. Clear game state management and transitions helped a lot not just in keeping structured event order, but in integrating with things like analytics and save systems.

- Cross functional teams. Hands down my favorite way to develop, working directly with all involved parties speeds up the iteration loop so much, and produces such a better product.

- Being smart with time and knowing when to go simple. Because we started working on the game before the Netflix controller application natively handled gyro data, we had to make our own during early development. While it's tempting to add all the bells and whistles, knowing this would be replaced, I opted for the solution that would get us 80% of the functionality, but for 20% of the time.

> Something something all percentages made up, I know.

### Things I'd do differently

- Plan for automation from day 1. The ability to run hundreds or thousands of sessions, even in extremely simplified manners, is increadibly useful when running tests on large amounts of sessions. It can quickly help things that may only crop up in every thousand or so cases. Catching these even earlier in development could have only been a good thing.

- Prefer asset validation at creation time vs CI or Runtime. Wrote a bunch of tests that helped with validation, and the delay in catching these was generally pretty small, but letting the user know *right* as the error is happening is much better UX.

<h2 style="text-align: left;" id="wrapup">Wrap Up</h2>
![Night School Logo](/assets/img/nss_logo.png)

As work wrapped up on Unhinged, I was offered the opportunity to lead a strike team to transition the studio to developing in Unreal Engine.

I partnered with production, built out a timeline, and we got to work.
We started with getting CI and source control setup using Perforce, UGS and Horde.

With many members from the team not having Unreal experience, I took the opportunity to make a setup tool that would install the required dependencies, create a workspace and sync the repo. All it asked of the user was for their name and email. 

This greatly sped up onboarding, as the strike team quickly expanded from a team of <10, to a team of 20+.
The studio was excited with the progress, and folks dove in enthusiastically.

We knew the tools and types of features we wanted available to us when the studio could devote full effort to prototyping our next project. We began work on these by making an iteration on the node based navigation from Unhinged, and using it to load in various level experiments and prototypes designers and engineers were building. We tagged them up using Gameplay Tags, and made a small scoring system and allowed designers to play with what was considered for scoring, and various unlocks/bonuses. Then as the player approcahed a doorway, we could select the best fit, and load the content.

Now with a flexible content load system developers could easily craft new experiements and prototypes, and introduce them into the game loop simply by creaing the required data asset.

This helped inform our content and level loading strategies, and run tests with various levels of graphical and computational cost.

We were both seeing what weird micro experiences could be crafted, in a strange warioware like manner, and determining how to organize and strucutre our games to deliver the highest visual fidelity we could, while hitting performance targets for the cloud streaming platform.

I spent a large amount of my time as Lead on the Unreal Strike team partnering with developers to help them onboard from Unity into Unreal Engine, and help them gain their footing in the new environment. This type of work is incredibly rewarding, and has helped me identify how much I like the management and mentorship side of this career field.

While I'm bummed we didn't get to continue on what was an awesome foundation, I'm grateful for my time at Night School, the things I learned, and the incredible developers I got to work with. Everyone showed up looking to help one another, and make the best game possible.

I am currently looking for work, and am interested in Senior / Lead Engineer and Engineering Manager roles.
